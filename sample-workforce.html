Geography

AI does not hit every place the same way

The same technology lands differently depending on what a place's jobs are, what they pay, and where its residents commute. That is why we report by state, county and labor market, not just nationally.

What people do

Industry mix sets how much work shrinks.

Counties with more office, retail and transportation jobs have more workers in declining occupations. Places built on federal, defense, science and health work have fewer.

What work pays

Wages set when AI pays first.

At today's AI cost, AI pays for the same tasks everywhere. If AI costs rise to $10–20 per hour of work, it pays first where labor costs most: in our sample state, the big-city suburbs and the metro core first, and the rural counties later.

Where people commute

Residents and jobs are not the same thing.

In one sample county, 39% of employed residents work in another state. A plan for residents has to follow the labor market they actually work in.

Absorption

Can the local economy absorb the change?

Overall, yes: growing occupations add more jobs than shrinking ones lose. The problem is the match. The jobs being lost and the jobs being created are different kinds of work.

Transitions

Will workers find similar pay, and how fast?

For every shrinking occupation we find the growing occupations whose skills fit best, how far the move is, what it pays, and which local programs, training providers and apprenticeships can carry workers across.

Direct

Most skills carry over; same or higher pay; under 6 months of training.

A short credential or apprenticeship is enough.

Winding

About half the skills carry over; about 90% of the pay; under 2 years.

A certificate or associate degree, ideally part-time and stackable.

Unpaved

Everything else: a longer climb.

Often a degree, but it can lead to much higher pay.

The capacity check

A good match only helps if there are jobs to fill.

We compare the workers headed to each destination with its new jobs, and steer plans toward occupations big enough to absorb them.

Recommendations

What to fund first

The brief ends with priorities a workforce board can act on this year.

  1. Short, stackable routes into care.

    Home health aides, nursing assistants and registered nurses add the most county jobs. Fund entry credentials that stack toward nursing.

  2. Trades through apprenticeship.

    About 660 new county jobs among electricians, plumbers, construction laborers, maintenance workers and equipment operators.

  3. IT support for office workers.

    Computer user support and network roles grow in the county; short routes fit adult office workers.

  4. Evening and online options.

    Most displaced workers are working adults; class schedules decide who can make the move.

  5. More local training providers.

    Only 5 Eligible Training Provider List programs are located in the county. Recruit providers for the growing occupations.

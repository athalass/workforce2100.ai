Can your economy absorb what AI changes?

An illustrative example for one county: who is affected, whether there are similar-paying jobs to move into, how hard the move is, and what to fund first.

Geography

AI does not hit every place the same way

The same technology lands differently depending on what a place's jobs are, what they pay, and where its residents commute. That is why we report by state, county and labor market, not just nationally.

What people do

Industry mix sets how much work shrinks.

Counties with more office, retail and administrative jobs have more workers in shrinking occupations. Places built on federal, defense, science and health work have fewer.

What work pays

Wages set when AI pays first.

At today's AI cost, AI pays for the same tasks everywhere. If AI costs rise to $10–20 per hour of work, it pays first where labor costs most: in our sample state, the big-city suburbs and the metro core first, and the rural counties later.

Where people commute

Residents and jobs are not the same thing.

In one sample county, 39% of employed residents work in another state. A plan for residents has to follow the labor market they actually work in.

Example county

Sample County at a glance

176,000 jobs. AI takes over about 17% of the work. 6.1% of jobs are in shrinking occupations, just below the state's 6.2%. Net gain of about 4,800 jobs (+2.7%), and about $2.4 billion a year in wages paid today for work AI takes over. Under a slow recovery, the county loses about 2,200 jobs on net.

Absorption

Can the local economy absorb the change?

Overall, yes: growing occupations add more jobs than shrinking ones lose. The problem is the match. The jobs being lost and the jobs being created are different kinds of work.

What the brief finds

Options for the county

  1. A smaller resilient core than the stateAbout 16% of private jobs are in industries that are resilient and absorb displaced workers, against 22% statewide.
  2. The largest exposure is a professional services clusterComputer systems design, consulting and engineering: about 13,000 jobs, location quotients above 2, and the steepest losses under slow recovery.
  3. The airport corridor is under-built for warehousingWarehousing has a location quotient of 0.4, against 1.8 for couriers and 3.6 for airport support. It absorbs 376 decent-paying jobs per 1,000 and grows under slow recovery: the clearest local expansion target.
  4. Home health care is a second gapLocation quotient 0.6, the fastest growth under slow recovery, and steady demand from an aging population.

Transitions

Will workers find similar pay, and how fast?

For every shrinking occupation we find the growing occupations whose skills fit best, how far the move is, what it pays, and which local programs, training providers and apprenticeships can carry workers across.

Direct

Most skills carry over; same or higher pay; under 6 months of training.

A short credential or apprenticeship is enough.

Winding

About half the skills carry over; about 90% of the pay; under 2 years.

A certificate or associate degree, ideally part-time and stackable.

Unpaved

Everything else: a longer climb.

Often a degree, but it can lead to much higher pay.

Recommendations

What to fund first

The brief ends with priorities a workforce board can act on this year, chosen to hold up under a slow recovery.

  1. Short, stackable routes into care.

    Home health aides, nursing and medical assistants and practical nurses have the largest gaps and the most growth, under both scenarios.

  2. Trades and technicians through apprenticeship.

    HVAC, auto and construction trades.

  3. Cross-industry routes for office workers.

    Workers in professional services, education and retail need routes into other industries, because their own industries will not re-employ them.

  4. Routes for bachelor's-level workers.

    Into health management, cybersecurity and data roles.

  5. Evening and online options.

    Most displaced workers are working adults; class schedules decide who can make the move.

About this sample

Illustrative. Sample State, Sample County and the example college are illustrative. Patterns come from a real state analysis. Names are withheld, and job, wage and completion counts are rescaled; percentages and relationships are unchanged.

Scenarios. Figures are estimates for 2035 under a main case. A slow-recovery stress test assumes only 75% of displaced work is replaced by new demand. Both are scenarios, not predictions.

Data. BLS QCEW 2025 and OEWS May 2025 (occupations, industries and wages); BLS Employment Projections 2025–35 (growth, separations and entry education); Census LODES (commuting); IPEDS 2023–24 completions linked to occupations through the NCES CIP 2020–SOC 2018 crosswalk; state eligible training provider and apprenticeship lists; state population projections. Task data from O*NET. AI usage and task-exposure research inform the model.

Limits. Robots and agentic AI beyond today's capabilities are not included. Military and most intelligence-agency jobs are outside standard employment data. Non-credit training is not counted in completions.

Not advice. Reports are general information, not legal, financial, investment, employment or academic-program advice.

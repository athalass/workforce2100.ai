Is the state ready for displaced workers?

There are enough openings overall. The weak points are mismatches by education level, by place and in training capacity, and they widen under a slow recovery.

Part 6 · Industries that protect

Which industries protect the state against a slow recovery?

An industry helps if it keeps growing when recovery is slow and if its new jobs are ones displaced workers can reach at decent pay. Many industries do one but not the other. The report scores about 240 industries on both.

The state's largest professional industries (computer systems design, consulting, engineering) fall in none of these groups. They are its most exposed: −9% to −10% under slow recovery, with fewer than 70 reachable jobs per 1,000.

What it means for recruitment and retention

Options for economic development and workforce agencies

  1. The best protection is already hereHealth care, the building trades and logistics are about a fifth of private jobs and keep growing under slow recovery. Retaining them, and staffing them from the displaced workforce, matters more than any single recruitment win.
  2. Common high-tech targets help less directlyData centers, semiconductors, aerospace and R&D pay well and create supplier jobs, but fewer than 130 of every 1,000 of their jobs are reachable for displaced workers. Pair them with training.
  3. Overlooked: electric power and machinery repairBoth absorb well, pay $34–45 an hour on average and grow nationally; electric power also supports about three supplier jobs per direct job.
  4. Growth is not the same as protectionFood service, hotels and building services add jobs under slow recovery but few that replace lost earnings.

What it means

Priorities for the state

The report closes with priorities a state can fund and track, and flags which ones hold up under a slow recovery.

  1. Build short, stackable routes into care, logistics and the trades.

    These add the most jobs in both the main case and the slow recovery.

  2. Create routes for bachelor's-level workers.

    Into health management, cybersecurity, data and clinical-adjacent roles.

  3. Fund curriculum redesign, not just new programs.

    Most completions are in programs where AI changes graduates' work more than it changes demand.

  4. Steer workers to destinations with room.

    Match transfer support to occupations with enough openings, not only the closest skill match.

  5. Close county training gaps.

    Especially in suburban counties with large office workforces.

  6. Retain and grow the industries that protect.

    Health care, the building trades and logistics are resilient and absorb displaced workers; recruitment of high-tech industries should come with training routes.

  7. Treat some expansions as provisional.

    Programs tied to manufacturing, logistics support and administrative services are flat under a slow recovery.

About this sample

Illustrative. Sample State, Sample County and the example college are illustrative. Patterns come from a real state analysis. Names are withheld, and job, wage and completion counts are rescaled; percentages and relationships are unchanged.

Scenarios. Figures are estimates for 2035 under a main case. A slow-recovery stress test assumes only 75% of displaced work is replaced by new demand. Both are scenarios, not predictions.

Data. BLS QCEW 2025 and OEWS May 2025 (occupations, industries and wages); BLS Employment Projections 2025–35 (growth, separations and entry education); Census LODES (commuting); IPEDS 2023–24 completions linked to occupations through the NCES CIP 2020–SOC 2018 crosswalk; state eligible training provider and apprenticeship lists; state population projections. Task data from O*NET. AI usage and task-exposure research inform the model.

Limits. Robots and agentic AI beyond today's capabilities are not included. Military and most intelligence-agency jobs are outside standard employment data. Non-credit training is not counted in completions.

Not advice. Reports are general information, not legal, financial, investment, employment or academic-program advice.

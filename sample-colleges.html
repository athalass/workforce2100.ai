State level

Sample State: five findings

About 700 occupations and 1.6 million jobs, 2025 to 2035. The state gains about 29,000 jobs on net, but about 93,000 workers are in occupations that shrink.

  1. Change is concentrated.

    Office and administrative support holds half of the jobs in declining occupations. Office, sales and transportation together hold three quarters. Secretaries, retail salespersons and customer service representatives lose the most jobs.

  2. Growth is in care, building and technology.

    Home health aides, nursing assistants, registered nurses, construction trades and computer occupations add the most jobs.

  3. Higher wages do not change where AI pays today.

    At today's AI cost, AI pays for about 47% of task-hours with state or U.S. wages alike. Wages matter only if AI costs $10–20 per hour of work. They do raise the stakes: about $21 billion a year in state wages is tied to the work hours AI removes.

  4. Most programs need new curriculum, not closure.

    Of 48,500 completions at 45 institutions, 22% are in programs to invest in, 77% in programs to reinvent and 1.6% in programs to review. In 85% of completions, AI can do half or more of graduates' task time.

  5. Pathways exist, but crowd onto a few occupations.

    For 74% of job losses, the best transfer route already has an in-state program, provider or apprenticeship. But the closest skill matches are small occupations; most displaced workers will need to move into care, trades and technology.

The program map

Every credential a college awards, placed by how demand for its graduates changes to 2035 and how much of their work AI can do. One picture tells a board where to grow, what to redesign and what to rethink.

Invest

Demand for graduates grows 10% or more by 2035.

Grow enrollment and capacity. If the program sits above the AI line, redesign it as it grows.

Reinvent

Demand changes between −10% and +10%.

Keep the program, but change what it teaches: directing AI, checking its work, and the human skills AI cannot do.

Review

Demand falls more than 10%.

Re-aim toward growing occupations, merge or wind down, after checking local placement. Programs whose range reaches zero are flagged uncertain.

The curriculum plan

How the programs need to change

Most programs keep their students but change what they teach. AI can do half or more of graduates' task time in most programs, so the graduate's job becomes directing that work, checking it and owning the result, plus the human work AI cannot do.

Five shifts for every high-AI program

  1. Teach delegation, not only doing.

    Build the four practices of AI fluency into every major: bounding (define the task and its limits), allocating (split work between person and AI), auditing (check AI output against evidence) and withholding (know when not to use AI). One required course early, then practice in every major course.

  2. Make checking a graded skill.

    About a quarter of the hours AI takes over still need a person to check and fix the work. Grade it with a judgment-delta assessment: an unaided attempt, an AI-assisted pass, an audit of AI work with planted errors, and a later unaided task.

  3. Practice on real, existing work.

    AI is weakest where context is messy. Move projects from blank-page assignments to existing systems, real organizations' data and community partners, in a teaching-clinic model.

  4. Grow the human core.

    A quarter to nearly half of graduates' task time is work AI cannot do: interviewing, observing, training people, coordinating teams, negotiating, physical care. Teach and assess it on purpose.

  5. Keep a person in charge where stakes are high.

    Where most work is high-stakes, teach accountability: who signs, what record is kept, which rules apply, and how to catch an AI error before it reaches a patient or client.

These shifts replace content rather than add credits. AI frees class time spent on drills and routine production; that time goes to delegation, checking, real work and the human core.

Computer science in detail: from writing code to owning agents

Teach vibe coding as the starting point, not the goal. A student who can only describe an app to AI builds demos on a blank page and fails in a real codebase. Employers need graduates who can specify, read, test, secure and answer for the code AI agents write.

Year 1

Build fast, then understand what was built

  • Vibe coding from week one: small apps built by describing them to an AI agent.
  • Every build followed by an unaided explanation: trace the code, predict its output, find a planted bug.
  • Core programming, data structures and algorithms stay, taught as the knowledge needed to judge AI code.

Year 2 · associate degree

Specify, test and review

  • Write specifications and tests before asking AI for code.
  • Code review as a graded skill, on AI changes with seeded flaws.
  • First brownfield project: add a feature to an existing codebase without breaking it.
  • Security of AI-generated code: dependencies, secrets, unsafe defaults.

After transfer · years 3–4

Own agents in real systems

  • Act as the named human owner of coding agents in legacy repositories.
  • System design, where AI is weakest: trade-offs, cost, maintainability.
  • Requirements work with real users, and a capstone in a software teaching clinic.

Roadmap

From plan to redesigned programs by 2030

A college can redesign every high-AI program in about three and a half years if it starts with a program-by-program task map this academic year.

  1. Nov 2026 – Jun 2027

    Map

    Each department lists what moves to AI and what grows in its graduates' jobs. Faculty complete AI fluency training. Add noncredit offerings to the inventory. Choose three pilots, one of each type. Record baselines.

  2. Aug 2027 – Jun 2028

    Pilot

    Launch the AI fluency course. Add judgment-delta assessments to two or three courses per pilot. Run one teaching clinic. Form employer panels. Decide on Review programs using local placement data.

  3. Jul 2028 – Jun 2030

    Redesign

    All high-AI programs adopt the five shifts through normal curriculum approval. Build stackable routes from noncredit into credit. Update transfer agreements with four-year partners.

  4. 2030 onward

    Review

    Re-run the occupation and program results every year with new federal data, and adjust programs when demand or AI exposure changes.

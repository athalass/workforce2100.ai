Sample findings · Maryland to 2035

What our reports find, and how they read

Excerpts from our Maryland study at three levels: the state, one county, and an institution's programs. Your report follows the same structure for your state, region or college.

State level

Maryland: five findings

703 occupations and 2.72 million jobs, 2025 to 2035. Maryland gains about 48,000 jobs on net, but about 155,000 workers are in occupations that shrink.

  1. Change is concentrated.

    Office and administrative support holds half of the jobs in declining occupations. Office, sales and transportation together hold three quarters. Secretaries, retail salespersons and customer service representatives lose the most jobs.

  2. Growth is in care, building and technology.

    Home health aides, nursing assistants, registered nurses, construction trades and computer occupations add the most jobs.

  3. Higher wages do not change where AI pays today.

    At today's AI cost, AI pays for about 47% of task-hours with Maryland or U.S. wages alike. Wages matter only if AI costs $10–20 per hour of work. They do raise the stakes: about $35 billion a year in Maryland wages is tied to the work hours AI removes.

  4. Most programs need new curriculum, not closure.

    Of 80,900 completions at 75 institutions, 22% are in programs to invest in, 77% in programs to reinvent and 1.6% in programs to review. In 85% of completions, AI can do half or more of graduates' task time.

  5. Pathways exist, but crowd onto a few occupations.

    For 74% of job losses, the best transfer route already has a Maryland program, provider or apprenticeship. But the closest skill matches are small occupations; most displaced workers will need to move into care, trades and technology.

County level

Anne Arundel County brief

293,000 jobs. 5.6% are in declining occupations, the 6th-lowest share of Maryland's 24 jurisdictions. Net gain of about 5,100 jobs; $3.8 billion a year in wages tied to the work hours AI removes.

What the brief adds

Context a county plan needs

  • Largest moversHome health aides (+1,460), managers (+850) and registered nurses (+820) grow most; secretaries (−1,620) and retail salespersons (−1,350) shrink most.
  • The real labor marketOnly 39% of employed residents work in the county. Plans should follow the Baltimore–Washington market, not county lines.
  • Population changeThe 65+ population grows 23%, driving care demand. Children under 5 rise 13% while school-age children fall slightly.
  • Training supplyGaps between growing occupations and Eligible Training Provider List programs located in the county.
